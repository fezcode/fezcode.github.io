## About

**Barry (2018–2023)** is HBO's four-season, thirty-two-episode series from Alec Berg and Bill Hader. Barry Berkman is a Midwestern hitman, a Marine who came home and kept killing for a living because it was the one thing he was good at. He follows a target into Gene Cousineau's acting class in Los Angeles and decides, on the spot, that this is who he would rather be. Henry Winkler plays Cousineau as a monstrous old ham, Sarah Goldberg plays Sally Reed as ambition with no floor under it, Stephen Root plays Fuches as the world's neediest handler, and Anthony Carrigan's NoHo Hank is the friendliest man in organised crime.

## The Comedy

The first two seasons are the best dark comedy of their decade. The joke is structural: an industry of people desperate to be seen versus a man whose entire livelihood depends on not being seen, and both halves played completely straight. Nobody in the acting class is stupid, they are just pointed at the wrong thing. Hank talks about a massacre in the register of a man organising a team offsite. *ronny/lily*, in season two, is twenty-six minutes of escalating physical catastrophe that stays funny the entire time it is getting people killed.

## The Camera Stays Put

This is the thing I loved most. Hader directs everything from season three onward and his instinct is to lock the camera off, hold the wide, and let you find the information yourself. Two people talk in the foreground while the actual event of the scene happens small and silent behind them — a body in a doorway, someone walking away who should not be leaving, a door closing on the other side of a hallway. No push in, no cut to the thing, no score telling you it matters. You either catch it or you don't, and the show trusts you enough not to check.

It is a grammar borrowed from Kubrick and the Coens, and it is doing real work rather than showing off. Static framing makes violence arrive without warning, because nothing in the shot braced for it. The long-lens highway chase in season three's *710N* is the flashy example everyone cites, but the quiet locked-off two-shots are where the show actually lives.

## Where It Lost Me

Somewhere in the back half, Barry stops being a man with a terrible job and becomes a psychopath, and the show is no longer asking whether he can change — it has already answered. The eight-year jump in season four seals it. The tone follows: by the end this is a horror film about a father, shot in flat desert daylight, with Barry as the thing in the house.

I understand the argument. The early seasons let you like him, and the late seasons make you pay for that, which is the whole design. It is honest, it is well made, and it is the correct ending for the story Hader was telling. I just preferred the version where the question was still open — the hitman in the acting class, not the monster in the hallway. Season four is admirable more often than it is enjoyable.

**4/5.** Two seasons of the funniest show on television, and two seasons of it explaining why that was never funny.
