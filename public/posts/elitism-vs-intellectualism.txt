Intellectualism is an appetite for finding out what is true and making it legible. Elitism is an appetite for rank that happens to use ideas as its currency.

They get confused because they inhabit the same buildings, cite the same journals, and compete for the same grants. But their incentives point in opposite directions. An intellectual treats the difficulty of a subject as friction to be reduced; if an equation or a mechanism can be stated more cleanly without losing precision, you state it more cleanly. An elitist treats difficulty as a moat. If an outsider can read the paper and follow the argument on the first pass, the moat isn't deep enough.

In science, that confusion isn't just an annoying personality flaw in faculty lounges. It actively slows down discovery and locks the public out of work the public largely paid for.

## 1. Obscurity as a Status Signal

Every technical field needs specialized vocabulary. Calling it *adenosine triphosphate* instead of "the cell's energy molecule" isn't snobbery; it is compression. It lets two biochemists speak without spending twenty minutes defining terms.

Elitism starts where compression ends and performance begins.

Read an average mid-tier journal abstract across almost any soft or hard science and count how much of the difficulty comes from the *phenomenon* versus how much comes from the *sentence structure*. Nominalizations piled four deep. Five-syllable Latinate verbs where a plain verb is more accurate. This isn't just a feeling: a 2017 analysis of more than 700,000 abstracts from 123 highly cited biomedical journals, spanning 1881 to 2015, found that readability has been steadily declining, driven largely by jargon and generic "science-ese."

To be fair, most dense prose is not a conspiracy. A lot of it comes from authors writing in their second or third language, from word limits that squeeze every idea into a nominalized lump, from inherited house style, and from what Steven Pinker calls the *curse of knowledge*: once you know something well, it becomes genuinely hard to imagine not knowing it.

But individual innocence doesn't make the system innocent. In an environment where hundreds of papers compete for scarce prestige, plain writing carries a risk: if you state your hypothesis and your method in clean, direct prose, a reader can immediately tell when the idea is trivial or the sample size is doing all the work. Dense prose works as camouflage whether or not anyone intended it to. It forces the reader to spend so much cognitive budget decoding the syntax that they have nothing left to audit the claim.

The irony is that obscurity doesn't even pay off in reach. A 2021 study of over 21,000 papers in cave research, a field notoriously fond of its own terminology, found that papers with more jargon in their titles and abstracts were cited *less*. None of the most highly cited papers had jargon in the title. Opacity may buy an aura of rigor inside a committee room, but it loses readers everywhere else.

When clarity is dismissed as "unsophisticated" and obscurity is mistaken for "rigor," a field stops selecting for people who understand things deeply and starts selecting for people who can imitate the liturgical register of the tribe.

## 2. The Tollbooth Economy

The second barrier isn't linguistic; it is structural.

The standard workflow of modern academic publishing would sound like a protection racket if you described it to someone outside the university system:

1. Public money funds much of the research, through government grants and public universities.
2. Scientists do the lab work and write the paper.
3. Other scientists peer-review and edit the paper for free.
4. A commercial publisher puts the finished PDF behind a $35–$50 per-article paywall, or charges university libraries millions in bundled subscriptions, and reports profit margins to match. In 2010, Elsevier's scientific publishing arm posted a 36% margin, higher than Apple, Google, or Amazon that year.

That arrangement survives because elitism makes it useful to the insiders. Journal prestige hierarchies ("impact factors") act as a sorting mechanism for hiring and tenure committees that don't have the time, or sometimes the range, to read a candidate's actual work. Where the paper appeared substitutes for what the paper proved.

The collateral damage is everyone outside the subscription perimeter: independent researchers, clinicians at underfunded hospitals, engineers in adjacent industries, students in developing countries, and patients trying to read the primary literature on their own diagnosis. Knowledge that was paid for as a public good gets converted into a private club credential.

People have been pushing back for decades. Physicists have posted preprints to arXiv since 1991. PLOS built a family of fully open journals. Plan S pushed European funders to require open publication, and from July 2025 the NIH requires the papers it funds to be freely available the moment they are published, with no embargo.

But the tollbooth didn't disappear; it moved. Under the "gold" open-access model, readers no longer pay, authors do, through article processing charges. When *Nature* opened that option in 2020, the fee was €9,500 per paper, and several Nature-branded journals now list prices above €10,000. That keeps the papers readable for everyone while making *publishing* in the most prestigious venues a privilege of well-funded labs. The gate just moved from the exit to the entrance.

## 3. Contempt for Translation

Watch how a department talks about a colleague who writes a clear textbook, maintains an open-source tool, or explains their field to the general public without watering down the math.

The polite word is *outreach*. The impolite word is *popularizer*, spoken with the exact inflection usually reserved for someone who left research to sell real estate.

There is a persistent, quiet belief in academic hierarchies that making knowledge reachable is janitor work: necessary perhaps for grant renewals, but beneath serious minds. The serious mind writes for the twelve other people in the sub-specialty and leaves the translation to someone lesser.

This gets the epistemology backwards. Translation is one of the hardest stress tests an idea can survive. When you try to explain a mechanism without leaning on your sub-field's private shorthand, every gap in your own causal model lights up at once. You find out which parts you actually understand and which parts you only knew the name of. Refusing to translate isn't protecting the purity of the science; half the time, it is protecting the author from finding out their explanation doesn't close.

## 4. What Gets Blocked at the Gate

The cost of scientific elitism isn't hurt feelings. It is missed work.

The first casualty is cross-disciplinary transfer. Some of the sharpest jumps in science happen when a method from one domain wanders into another: statistical physics into neuroscience, computer science into protein folding, control theory into immunology. When every sub-field writes in an impenetrable dialect and locks its papers in separate silos, two researchers solving the exact same mathematical problem under different names can work ten years apart without ever realizing it.

Reproducibility rots next. When prestige is tied to novel, flashy claims in high-rank journals rather than clean, open datasets and readable methods sections, replication becomes a thankless chore. The gate protects the credential, not the finding.

And finally, the public is left in an epistemic vacuum. People do not stop wanting to understand medicine, climate, nutrition, or technology just because the primary literature locked them out and talked down to them. When real researchers treat public comprehension as beneath them, grifters with a microphone and a podcast gladly fill the room. You cannot build a wall around expertise for fifty years and then act surprised when the public stops trusting the people behind the wall.

---

## The Priesthood and the Method

The entire point of the scientific revolution was to replace a priesthood with a method. Truth stopped being whatever a credentialed authority declared in Latin and became whatever survived contact with a public, repeatable experiment.

When modern institutions lock papers behind paywalls, bury plain mechanisms under defensive prose, and treat public understanding as an afterthought, they are rebuilding the priesthood they were supposed to tear down.

Science should be for everyone. Not as watered-down charity handed out from an ivory tower, and not because every reader is going to run a particle accelerator in their garage, but because reality is shared property. Anyone willing to put in the attention to follow the evidence deserves a clear path to the source.

Whenever a piece of scientific writing, an institution, or an expert makes a real concept harder to reach than the concept itself requires, that isn't rigor. That is status anxiety wearing a lab coat.

> **Lesson:** Difficulty that comes from the universe is science. Difficulty added by the author is rent-seeking.
