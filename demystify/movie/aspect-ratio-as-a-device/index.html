WHAT IT IS

Treating the frame's proportions as a variable rather than a fixed format. A film may switch between ratios to separate timelines, open up for a set piece, or squeeze to a near-square box to make a world feel enclosed. Ratios carry inherited associations: 1.37 reads as archival, 1.85 as ordinary, 2.39 as epic, and a film can borrow those associations simply by changing shape.

WHO REACHES FOR IT

Wes Anderson gives each of the three time periods in The Grand Budapest Hotel its own ratio, so the audience is told which decade they are in before a word is spoken. Joel Coen shoots The Tragedy of Macbeth in a boxy 1.19 to press the frame around its actors. Paweł Pawlikowski keeps Cold War in 4:3 to trap two people inside the same narrow rectangle. Zack Snyder released Justice League in 4:3 to preserve a composition intended for tall screens.

TRIVIA

The Grand Budapest Hotel uses 1.37:1 for its 1930s sections, 2.35:1 for the 1960s and 1.85:1 for the 1980s, matching each era to the format that era actually shot in. The joke is archival accuracy used as a wayfinding device, and it works on audiences who could not name a single one of those numbers.

WHAT IT DOES TO YOU

Shape is felt rather than read. A narrower frame crowds a face and makes a room feel shorter; a wider one isolates a figure in landscape. Because the change happens at a cut, it can mark a transition more cleanly than any dissolve, and because almost nobody consciously tracks ratios, the information arrives without announcing itself.

HOW IT IS DONE

Mostly a decision about framing charts and monitoring, since modern sensors are cropped rather than reshot. The complication is downstream: every deliverable, from a cinema DCP to a streaming master, has to carry the changes intact, and a careless conversion will letterbox or crop them into uniformity.

WHAT IT COSTS

Cheap to shoot and awkward to distribute. Television and streaming platforms have historically fought variable ratios, some players stretch or zoom automatically, and phone viewing punishes a boxy frame. Several films with deliberate ratio changes have shipped flattened versions, which is why directors who care about it tend to say so loudly.
