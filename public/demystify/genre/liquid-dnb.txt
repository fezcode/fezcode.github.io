what:
 Drum & bass split off from jungle around 1994–95 when producers began smoothing the ragga edges into something warmer. LTJ Bukem's Good Looking label is the origin point; the "liquid" name got fixed by Fabio's *Liquid Funk* compilation in 2000. The 2010s brought a second wave via Hospital Records and the Canadian label Monstercat, which is where Feint sits — big topline vocals, cinematic string pads, and a strict 172–176 BPM.

artists:
 LTJ Bukem, Fabio, Calibre, High Contrast, Netsky, Hospital Records' roster (London Elektricity, Danny Byrd), and the Monstercat/gaming-adjacent wing: Feint, Koven, Camellia. *Feint* is a UK producer whose Monstercat singles — "Snake Eyes" (2015) with vocalist CoMa among them — travelled far beyond DnB via osu!, Rocket League and Twitch playlists.

trivia:
 The genre's entire rhythmic DNA is one bar: the *Amen break*, six seconds of drums from The Winstons' 1969 B-side "Amen, Brother." Drummer G.C. Coleman died broke in the 1990s having never received a penny for it. Liquid producers usually re-layer the break with cleaner one-shots so it reads as texture rather than the ragged chop of 1993 jungle.

sonic:
 Two independent tempo layers running at once: a sub-bass and a half-time-feeling melodic line at ~87 BPM, and breakbeats at 174. Reverb tails are long and bright, the sub is sine-clean rather than growling, and vocals sit high and forward with heavy delay throws. Almost no midrange grit — that's what separates liquid from neurofunk.

gear:
 Amen/Think break samples, Roland TR-909 hats layered over chopped breaks, Reese-style bass (a detuned saw pair originally from Kevin Saunderson's "Just Want Another Chance"). Modern liquid is nearly all in-the-box: Serum and Massive for the sub, Valhalla or Lexicon-modelled reverbs, iZotope/FabFilter multiband on the drum bus.

prod:
 Mixed for the *drop-into-space* contrast: 16 bars of sparse pads and vocal, then full break plus sub. Because the sub carries the melody, the kick is usually short and midrange-heavy so it punches through without fighting the bass. Monstercat-era liquid is mastered loud and bright for headphone and stream playback rather than a soundsystem, which is why it reads as glossy next to a Good Looking record from 1996.
