Nobody Walks Away: A Short Rant About Three HBO Max Crime Shows

Sıfır Bir, The Penguin and Barry, watched back to back on HBO Max. Three completely different shows arriving at the same verdict: crime is a debt with compounding interest, nobody gets an exit ramp, and the bill is always handed to whoever nearby still loves you.

⚠️ Spoilers for all three shows. The endings especially. That is the whole subject.

Nobody Walks Away: A Short Rant About Three HBO Max Crime Shows

HBO Max put them in a row on my home screen like it was doing me a favour: Sıfır Bir , The Penguin , Barry . I watched them in roughly that order. I gave them 5, 5 and 4.

And all three let me down in exactly the same way.

Not badly made — they are three of the best-made things on the service. Let me down in the specific sense that I sat through all of it waiting for one single person to get out, and not one of them does. Three different countries' worth of tone, one verdict.

1. Sıfır Bir: loyalty is the murder weapon

Savaş, Cio and Özgür, the Hürriyet neighbourhood, Adana, six seasons. The show's actual thesis is that every act of loyalty creates the next funeral, and the characters know this. They say it out loud. They do it anyway, because walking away from your friends is the one move nobody in that neighbourhood was ever handed a script for.

So the body count is not a plot escalation, it's an interest payment. Somebody gets stabbed for somebody else's debt, and the debt does not go away, it just changes hands. Six seasons later the neighbourhood is exactly where it was in episode one, minus a generation of young men. Nobody got rich. Nobody moved to İstanbul and opened a garage. The street is the street.

2. The Penguin: winning is the punishment

Oz Cobb gets everything. Penthouse, top hat, Gotham laid out under the window. It is the only one of the three where the protagonist straightforwardly succeeds, and it is the most miserable ending of the lot.

Because look at the receipt. Two brothers drowned as children, on purpose, so he could be the only one left to love. Sofia Falcone posted back to Arkham for a thing she didn't do. His mother kept alive in a chair specifically so she can witness the empire, then not even that — she gets a stroke halfway through telling him she hates him, so he hires a girl to dress up as her and hold the fantasy instead. And Vic, the one clean thing in eight hours of television, strangled on a bench mid-sentence for the crime of saying "you're family to me" and meaning it.

The final shot is a man standing in the exact spot he spent forty years clawing toward, in a room with nobody real in it. Then the Bat-Signal comes up behind him like an invoice.

3. Barry: the change never arrives

This is the one that had the most hope in it, which is why it stings worst. The premise is literally a killer who wants to be someone else. Four seasons, thirty-two episodes, and the answer is no.

He doesn't go out in a blaze either. He gets shot mid-sentence, on a carpet, by a broken old man, in flat ugly daylight — a death with no shape to it. And then the show does the cruellest thing in any of these three endings: the epilogue. A terrible biopic where Barry is the misunderstood hero, Gene Cousineau is the villain doing the time for it, and Barry's son watches the lie and looks proud. He couldn't become a better man while alive, so the industry does it for him in post.

What I am actually annoyed about

It's that they agree with each other. Three shows with nothing in common — a YouTube series shot by locals in Adana, a Batman spin-off, a Hollywood satire — arriving independently at the same finding:

Crime is not a ladder. It's a debt with compounding interest, and the interest is always paid by whoever nearby still loves you.

Not one of these men pays for himself. Savaş's loyalty kills his friends. Oz's ambition kills the kid who trusted him. Barry's redemption arc costs Gene his freedom and hands his own son a fabricated father. The protagonist survives the longest precisely because everyone around him is absorbing the cost.

And the genre trained me to expect an exit ramp! There is always supposed to be one guy who takes the deal, or gets to Sicily, or ends up bored in witness protection complaining about the noodles. HBO Max apparently does not stock that ending. You get the room, exactly as bad as you found it, and the only variable is your headcount on the way out.

The letdown is the point, which is the most annoying part

I know. I know they're right. A crime story where somebody escapes clean is a fantasy about consequences being optional, and all three of these shows would rather be honest than let you off. That's why they're good. That's why two of them got a 5 out of me.

It just means you cannot watch three of them in a fortnight without developing a small personal grievance against the streaming service that queued them up.

Would it kill anyone, once, to let a guy open a restaurant.

Lesson: The genre's real subject isn't whether crime pays. It's who gets handed the bill — and it's never the man who ran up the tab.
