The Death of "Arkadaş": A Retrospective on How Turks Stopped Caring and Started Generalizing

A linguistic rant on how an ancient steppe combat pact ("arka-taş") devolved into hollow corporate jargon, paired with a chronological survey of what Seljuks, Ottomans, and modern street-dwellers called each other when precision still mattered.

Or: How an ancient steppe military pact got downgraded to corporate email filler, and what our ancestors used before we gave up on precision.

TL;DR

In Turkish, everyone now calls everyone "arkadaş". It has suffered catastrophic semantic bleaching. A word forged on the Eurasian steppe to describe the comrade literally pressing his back (arka) against yours in battle has been hollowed out into a passive-aggressive corporate greeting, a call-center bot placeholder, and a shout from a speeding taxi.

Turkish used to have one of the most delightfully stratified vocabularies for human connection in history. Below is a taxonomy of what Turks actually called each other across three eras—Seljuk, Ottoman, and Modern Day—before we abandoned precision for a single, limp, multipurpose syllable pack.

The Indictment: Anatomy of Semantic Bleaching

Let's establish the crime before examining the historical evidence.

Etymologically, arkadaş stems from the Old Turkic root arka (back, rear, support, protector) combined with the comitative/associative suffix -daş (denoting mutual sharing of the base noun, as in yoldaş or karındaş) [1] [2] [3] . In steppe warfare and nomad folklore, it referred to the fellow horseman who literally pressed his back against yours during an ambush so you couldn't be struck from your blind spot, or the comrade leaning against the same rock while dividing rations on the warpath. It was a mutual life-insurance policy sealed with sweat, arrowheads, and marrow.

Look at where that word lives today:

  • An HR manager wraps an eviction notice in velvet: "İlgili arkadaşımızın dikkatine."
  • A minibus driver cutting three lanes in Mecidiyeköy screams out the window: "Arkadaşım baksana ya!" at a pedestrian he would gladly turn into paste.
  • A telecom IVR informs you that "uygun bir müşteri temsilcisi arkadaşımız" will answer within 45 minutes.

When a single noun can simultaneously describe your childhood soulmate, a faceless bureaucrat, and a man trying to tow your Honda Civic, the word is dead. It conveys zero emotional data, zero social hierarchy, and zero genuine affection.

The tragedy is that our ancestors were linguistic snobs about companionship. They had specific words for the road, the womb, the guild, the hearth, the tavern, and the back-alley.

1. The Seljuk & Steppe Era: Roads, Blood, and Mystics

In early Anatolia and the Central Asian steppes, you didn't have casual acquaintances. Life was geographically sparse and violently short. A companion was either bound to you by shared biology, a shared caravan route, or a sacred oath.

The Lexicon:

  • Yoldaş (The Fellow Traveler / Comrade) [1] [2]
    Long before 20th-century political ideology claimed the term, yoldaş (yol + -daş) was nomadic sacred law. Recorded in Kaşgarlı Mahmud's Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk (1072), crossing the Silk Road or the Anatolian plateaus meant sharing water bags, facing wolves, and surviving blizzards. A yoldaş was someone whose survival was structurally entangled with yours. If you abandoned a yoldaş, no tribe between Samarkand and Konya would take you in.
  • Karındaş / Qardaş (Sharing the Same Womb) [1] [2]
    The ancestral root of kardeş (karın + -daş, literally "sharing the same belly"). Calling someone outside your immediate kin karındaş in old Turkic epics (such as the Book of Dede Korkut) was an honorary blood pact—a declaration that you owed them tribal vengeance and protection.
  • Ahi (The Guild Brother) [3] [4] [5]
    Born out of the Anatolian Ahilik institution (intertwining Old Turkic akı [generous, open-handed] with Arabic akhī [my brother]). Recorded by Ibn Battuta in his 14th-century Rihla, an Ahi was not just a friend; he was a comrade in economic ethics and urban defense: his door was open, his table was open, his pocket was open, but his eyes and tongue were sealed against dishonor.
  • Erenler / Yârân (The Initiated Companions) [1]
    Derived from Old Turkic er- / ermek (to attain, reach spiritual maturity). Used among Sufi dervishes and traditional Anatolian hearth assemblies (Yaran Meclisi). When Yunus Emre or Hacı Bektaş-ı Veli addressed a room as Erenler, they weren't greeting an audience—they were acknowledging that everyone present had submitted their ego to the same spiritual furnace.

2. The Ottoman Era: The Empire of Nuanced Hierarchy

The Ottoman Empire was an enormous machinery of cosmopolitan trade, courtly etiquette, craft guilds, and neighborhood policing. As a consequence, Ottoman address forms abandoned steppe survivalism in favor of hyper-specific social calibration.

You never addressed a scholar, an equal, a tavern mate, a fellow townsman, and a neighborhood tough with the same vocabulary.

A. The Cultivated & High-Register

  • Azizim (My Precious / Dear One) [6] [7]
    From Arabic 'Azīz (rare, valuable, mighty). Documented in Sir James Redhouse's Lexicon, this was reserved for educated gentlemen and epistolary correspondence. It expressed deep reverence without the icy distance of formal administrative titles.
  • Mîrim (My Bey / My Prince) [6] [7]
    A Persian contraction (Mīr-i men). Standard in Divan poetry (Bâkî, Nedîm) and salon culture, used playfully and courteously among equals to elevate the listener. It is the verbal equivalent of tipping a velvet fez.
  • Refik / Refika (The Equal Companion) [6]
    From Arabic r-f-q (gentleness, peerage, accompanying; see Şemseddin Sami's Kāmûs-ı Türkî). A refik was an intellectual equal, a travel partner, or a lifelong collaborator (Refik-i hayat for a spouse). You did not call a subordinate refik.

B. The Surgical "Hem-" (Co-) Prefix Suite

Ottoman Turkish adopted Persian hem- (fellow / co-) and turned it into an ultra-precise classification system [6] [7] :

  • Hemşehri: One who shares your hometown or province (the ancestor of modern hemşerim).
  • Hemhâl: A soul sharing your exact emotional or existential distress.
  • Hempa: Literally same-foot (hem + pā)—someone walking at the exact same stride; an accomplice, comrade-in-arms, or partner in mischief.
  • Hembezm: A companion of the feast or tavern table (bezm).

C. The Mahalle, the Coffeehouse & the Kabadayı

  • Ahbap (The Intimates) [3] [6]
    Technically the Arabic broken plural of habib (beloved ones). Over centuries in Istanbul, it distilled into the classic title for the circle of trusted companions with whom you broke bread and shared life (as chronicled in Ahmet Rasim's Şehir Mektupları).
  • Gözüm (My Eye) [8]
    The quintessential neighborhood vocative. Documented in Reşat Ekrem Koçu's İstanbul Ansiklopedisi, calling someone gözüm meant you guarded them with the same involuntary reflex you use to protect your own eyes from dust.
  • Canım Ciğerim (My Soul, My Liver) [8]
    In classical Galenic-Islamic medicine and Ottoman folk idioms, the liver (ciğer) was the anatomical seat of courage, passion, and deep visceral attachment. If someone was your ciğer, they occupied real estate inside your chest cavity.

3. Modern Turkey: The Fragmented Chaos

When the Ottoman guild structure dissolved and 20 million Anatolians migrated into concrete apartment towers, dolmuş routes, and open-plan tech offices, Turkish address forms mutated into modern functional tribes.

Instead of one unified register, we splintered into specialized vocatives:

The Prescription

Here is the field rule:

  1. If you are fighting side-by-side in life or career, call him Yoldaş or Tertip.
  2. If he is your partner in questionable schemes, call him your Hempa.
  3. If you respect his intellect and company over raki or coffee, revive Refik or Azizim.
  4. If you are debugging code or playing five-a-side football on a Wednesday night, stick to Hocam.

Just stop calling the tow-truck driver or your passive-aggressive coworker "arkadaşım." He is not your back-stone. He is not guarding your flank from Byzantine archers. Name the reality.
