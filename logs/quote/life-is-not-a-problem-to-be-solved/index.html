"Life is not a problem to be solved, but a reality to be experienced."

by J. J. van der Leeuw · misattributed to Søren Kierkegaard

The discovery

Life is not a problem to be solved

"Life is not a problem to be solved, but a reality to be experienced."

The attribution is wrong

Circulates asSøren Kierkegaard (1813–1855)
Actually fromJ. J. van der Leeuw (1893–1934)
WorkThe Conquest of Illusion, Alfred A. Knopf, London & New York, 1928
LocationChapter One, "The Quest for Life" — section "The Mystery of Life"
As printed"The mystery of life is not a problem to be solved; it is reality to be experienced."

The sentence is not in Kierkegaard. Not in the journals, not in the pseudonymous authorship, not in the signed works. Nobody who attributes it ever produces a page number, which is the usual tell — the quote circuit is generous with names and stingy with citations, and a line that has been floating for eighty years without a single source behind it has generally never had one.

What it does have is an author, and he is not Danish, not an existentialist, and not dead by 1855.

The chain

Three steps, and only one of them is a mistake.

  1. 1928. Van der Leeuw prints the sentence in The Conquest of Illusion.
  2. 1948. The Hibbert Journal (vols. 46–47, p. 237) refers to it as "the famous Kierkegaardian slogan." In 1948 that reads as an adjective of resemblance — Kierkegaard-like, the way "Kafkaesque" describes a situation Kafka never wrote.
  3. 1976. Robert A. Hipkiss, Jack Kerouac: Prophet of the New Romanticism, p. 83, prints it as Kierkegaard's, flatly. From there it enters the quotation trade and never leaves.

The error happens between 2 and 3: an adjective of resemblance gets read as an adjective of authorship. "Kierkegaardian" stops meaning this sounds like him and starts meaning he said it. It is a small, entirely reasonable misreading, and it is the single most productive mechanism in the whole business of fake quotations.

A note on what is verified here and what isn't. The van der Leeuw text was checked against the book itself, and the wording below is transcribed from it. The 1948 and 1976 waypoints are the earliest instances anyone has produced, taken on the strength of that record rather than firsthand — they establish that the misattribution is younger than the sentence, which is the load-bearing claim, but the trail may well run back further than 1948.

The word that got deleted

The circulating version is not a paraphrase. It is the original with its subject removed.

Van der Leeuw"The mystery of life is not a problem to be solved…"
Internet"Life is not a problem to be solved…"

Three words, and the claim changes species.

Van der Leeuw is not saying that life is not a problem. He is saying that one specific thing — the mystery of life, the residue left over after every soluble question has been answered — is not a problem. He has no objection to problems. His book is full of them and he works at them methodically for 160 pages. His claim is narrower and much stranger than the poster version: that there exists a category of thing which is not hard to solve but not the kind of thing solving applies to, and that mistaking the second for the first is the characteristic error of the modern intellect.

Strip the qualifier and you get a general licence to stop thinking. Keep it and you get a boundary claim: here is the edge of what analysis reaches. Those are not the same sentence, and only one of them was written.

What the book is doing when it says this

The line appears twice, and the placement is deliberate. It opens the section:

"The mystery of life is not a problem to be solved; it is reality to be experienced. Beware of the man who claims to have solved the problem of life, who would explain all its complexities and, with deadly logic, build a system in which all the facts of our existence may be pigeon-holed and neatly stored away. He stands condemned by his own claim."

Then two pages of argument, and then the same sentence closes it, this time with the indefinite article the internet kept:

"…none may tell the final mystery — its name is experience.

The mystery of life is not a problem to be solved, it is a reality to be experienced."

Statement, argument, restatement. It is a thesis with a proof between its two printings, not an aphorism — which is precisely what the quotation trade discards when it lifts a sentence out of a book.

What sits between them is a claim about knowledge, not about lifestyle. Van der Leeuw's target is systematic philosophy: the intellectual structure that "has the appearance of a philosophy of life, but is in reality a phantasm of death." Life, he argues, is movement, and a system is a snapshot; "were it possible to contain life in a system it would no longer be life, but death." So the mystery is not reachable by logic — not because logic is weak, but because it is the wrong instrument, the way a ruler is the wrong instrument for weight. It is reachable, he thinks, only through direct mystical experience, which he calls entering "the world of the Real."

That is the sentence's actual home: a theosophical epistemology, in a book dedicated to J. Krishnamurti and the memory of his brother Nityananda, arguing for cosmic consciousness as a source of knowledge and against Kant's ban on knowing the thing in itself. Nothing in it is existentialist. Nothing in it is even secular.

Who wrote it

Jacobus Johannes van der Leeuw (Rotterdam, 26 August 1893 — Tanganyika, 23 August 1934) was a Dutch theosophist. Doctorate in 1920; sailed to Australia in 1924 for occult training under C. W. Leadbeater; ordained in the Liberal Catholic Church and made treasurer of the Manor, the Theosophical Society's villa in Sydney; president of the Society's Dutch Section, 1930–31; later a lecturer in the United States and a field organiser for the New Education Fellowship.

In 1934 the Fellowship invited him to speak at a conference in Johannesburg. He bought a De Havilland Leopard Moth for the trip and flew it himself. On the return leg he flew into a mountain in Tanganyika and was killed, three days short of his forty-first birthday.

He is not otherwise famous. His one durable sentence is famous under somebody else's name.

Why it stuck to Kierkegaard

Because it is not a bad fit — which is the whole problem. Misattributions survive on plausibility, and this one is plausible enough that correcting it feels pedantic.

Kierkegaard's central quarrel, the one that occupies the Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846), is with Hegel's System, and the objection runs close to van der Leeuw's: a logical system is possible; an existential system is impossible. You cannot build a structure that contains an existing person, because the person is still going, and the system has to hold still to be a system. Existence outruns any account of it.

And he had said something adjacent at twenty-two. The Gilleleje journal entry of 1 August 1835, written on holiday in north Zealand while he tried to work out what to do with himself:

"…the thing is to find a truth which is truth for me, to find the idea for which I am willing to live and die… What use would it be to discover a so-called objective truth… if it had no deeper meaning for myself and my life?"

And in 1843, the line that actually is his, and which gets mangled in a different way:

"It is quite true what philosophy says: that life must be understood backwards. But then one forgets the other principle: that it must be lived forwards."

So Kierkegaard is a reasonable guess. He is simply the wrong answer.

And the resemblance breaks at the interesting point. Both men say the intellect cannot get you there; they disagree completely about what does. Van der Leeuw's exit from the intellect goes up and out — mystical experience, cosmic consciousness, a Reality we are each an infinitesimal part of, available to anyone willing to climb the mountain. Kierkegaard's exit is faith, and faith in his account is not a wider kind of knowing. It is a leap made in fear and trembling, alone, without consolation, and with no guarantee that anything is on the other side. One is an invitation to a larger awareness. The other is Abraham on the road to Moriah with a knife.

Put the sentence in Kierkegaard's mouth and it turns reassuring. That is the surest sign it was never his.

The trap in the short version

Stripped, the line is used to end arguments: stop overthinking, stop analysing, just live. It has become a way of scoring a point without making one — unanswerable, because any objection can be waved off as more of the analysis it condemns.

Note what the book does instead. Van der Leeuw arrives at "not a problem to be solved" by argument, at the end of a chapter of careful reasoning, and then goes on reasoning for ten more chapters. He is describing where thinking runs out, from the inside, having gone there. Quoting the sentence to avoid the thinking inverts it: the claim is that the mystery survives your best analysis, not that you are excused from attempting one.

Which is also the fairest thing to say about the misattribution. The people passing the line around under Kierkegaard's name have taken a sentence about the limits of intellectual work as permission to skip the intellectual work — starting with the ten seconds it takes to ask whether he wrote it.

See also

  • J. J. van der Leeuw, The Conquest of Illusion (Knopf, 1928), ch. 1 — the primary source, and the only place the sentence appears with its subject intact.
  • Gabriel Marcel — the rigorous version of the same distinction: a mystery is "a problem which encroaches upon its own data." A problem sits outside you and can be examined; a mystery has you inside it, so the questioner cannot be separated from the question. Marcel's examples are being, the union of body and soul, freedom, and love.
  • Frank Herbert, Dune (1965) — the Reverend Mother Gaius Helen Mohiam, to Paul: "The mystery of life isn't a problem to solve, but a reality to experience." Herbert kept the three words everyone else dropped.
  • "Life is not a problem to be solved, but a mystery to be lived" — a third variant, usually hung on Thomas Merton and sometimes on Kierkegaard as well, with the same absence of a citation behind it. The sentence appears to have escaped and started breeding.
  • Kierkegaard, Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846) — the real thing, and considerably less comfortable than the slogan he gets credited with.
