## About

**True Detective (2014)** is an anthology crime drama created by Nic Pizzolatto for HBO — but this entry is about the first season only, which is the one that matters. Louisiana State Police detectives Rust Cohle (Matthew McConaughey) and Marty Hart (Woody Harrelson) work a ritualistic murder in 1995, and seventeen years later are hauled back in to explain it. The whole eight episodes are told through that 2012 interrogation, which keeps quietly contradicting the story the two men agreed to tell. Cary Joji Fukunaga directed every episode; Pizzolatto wrote every episode alone, without a writers' room.

## Why It Works

The premise is a police procedural; the season is anything but. The case is a pretext for a much older question — what kind of person does this work make you, and what do you have to believe about the universe to keep doing it. Cohle's nihilism and Hart's ordinary, self-serving decency aren't a philosophical debate bolted onto a thriller; they *are* the thriller, and the plot exists mostly to grind them against each other until something gives.

McConaughey and Harrelson are both at the absolute top of their powers here. Fukunaga directing all eight episodes gives it something almost no prestige television has — a single sustained mood, start to finish, with Louisiana rendered as a sort of drowned, post-industrial underworld of refineries, flood plains, and abandoned schools. And the six-minute unbroken tracking shot through the Hoyt housing project at the end of "Who Goes There" is still the best-staged action sequence ever put on television.

## The Theme

Time, and whether it does anything but circle. "Time is a flat circle" became a meme, but the season means it: the past keeps surfacing inside the present, the same crimes get committed by the same kinds of men, the institutions always close ranks, and the detectives arrive far too late to save anyone who mattered. The ending is the whole argument in two lines of dialogue — the dark has more territory, but it's still losing. That's about as much optimism as this show is willing to extend, and it's earned every inch of it.

A permanent **5/5**. Eight perfect episodes.
